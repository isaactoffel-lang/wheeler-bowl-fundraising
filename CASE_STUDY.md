# Wheeler Bowl case study

[Project overview](README.md) · [Publication scope](PUBLICATION.md)

## Coordinate the work between replies

Fundraising involves more than sending a request and recording a positive response. An offer can still require collection, clarification, preparation, and a handoff to the person responsible for the event.

The Wheeler Bowl project documents that operational chain and includes an auction interface prototype. The public case study focuses on the workflow and the boundary between a prototype and an operational service.

## Distinguish the records

An outreach record answers what was requested and what needs followup. A confirmation record answers what was offered. A collection record answers whether the gift has arrived. An event handoff answers whether the item is ready for its intended use.

Treating those as interchangeable creates avoidable uncertainty. A positive response can be interpreted as a collected gift, or a collected gift can still be unclear to the person preparing the event.

The workflow documentation and blank tracking templates provide places for those responsibilities to be made explicit. The public writeup does not expose the working records themselves.

## Review a handoff

| Question | Why it matters |
| --- | --- |
| What has actually been offered? | A response should not be interpreted more broadly than the commitment. |
| Has the gift been received? | Event preparation should not depend on an assumed collection. |
| What still needs followup? | Open work needs to remain visible after a positive response. |
| Who is responsible for the next handoff? | The record should support action rather than leave ownership implicit. |
| Is the item ready for the event? | Receipt and readiness are related but distinct questions. |

These are workflow review questions. Their presence does not establish that a particular donor commitment has been completed.

## What the auction prototype proves

The prototype explores how available items could be presented and how an auction interface could behave. It is useful for reviewing the shape of the experience before treating it as an operational service.

It does not process payments or provide a shared bidding backend. An interaction in that interface must therefore remain separate from an actual financial transaction or a reliable shared auction record.

The public description keeps that boundary visible. Presenting the prototype as a completed fundraising platform would imply capabilities that the project does not establish.

## Design tradeoffs

More explicit workflow stages can improve a handoff, but the working record still needs to be maintained. A detailed template cannot guarantee that collection status is current.

Keeping the prototype separate avoids confusing interface work with operational readiness. It also makes the remaining requirements easier to discuss without relying on the appearance of the screen.

The public version omits donor contacts, gift details, values, bids, and operational tracking. This limits what a reader can inspect about the event itself while preserving a useful explanation of the coordination problem.

## Contribution and evidence

The private project contains workflow documentation, blank tracking templates, and an auction prototype adapted from the original event preparation work. AI assisted with adaptation, checks, and documentation.

This editorial review covers the public explanation, navigation, and publication boundary. It does not verify collection status, donor commitments, payment handling, or fundraising outcomes.

Source, working records, screenshots, and development history are retained privately.
